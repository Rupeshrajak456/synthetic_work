Steps 1 to 3

Reading a quarter of the story first

Before the whole text was read, three sampled blocks shaped a first impression, a research problem and a framework. This page records that sequence, including where the first impression was right and where it had to change.

Step 1

Partial reading

Sampling rule. Split the text into equal parts, then read a block of about 20% of each part: the first, the middle, the last, or a random block.

Reproducible. The middle block was chosen at random with seed 42, so anyone running the notebook again reads the same lines.

Model followed: partial_reading.txt from the Wife's Letter project.

The story was split into 487 sentence-like units, breaking at line ends and after full stops, question marks and exclamation marks. The units were cut into three parts of 162, 162 and 163. From each part one block of 32 units was read: the opening block of Part 1, a random block from Part 2, and the closing block of Part 3. Together these came to 2,169 of the story's 9,122 words, about 24%.

While reading, I marked phrases that seemed to carry the story's concerns. Each one was then checked against its block, so that nothing was recorded from memory or from outside the sample. All 30 marked phrases were found in their blocks.

Step 2, part one

First impression

Source file
initial_impression.md

The sampled passages suggested a story about observation, mystery and the interpretation of physical evidence. The opening block introduces uncertainty through an abandoned handbag that has been forced open. The random middle block brings in the casket, the missing pearls and a run of burglaries. The closing block turns to the physical construction of the casket and a concealed message. Across the three, Thorndyke's examination connects objects and events that at first seem separate.

The narrative appeared to move from uncertainty and suspicion towards explanation and discovery. That was an impression from samples, not a conclusion about the whole story, and it was written down before the full read so that it could later be tested rather than remembered.

One overall concept: material evidence and deductive revelation. Ordinary physical objects can carry hidden information that becomes meaningful through observation, examination and interpretation.Initial impression, recorded after the partial reading

Step 2, part two

Positionality and reflexivity

Why this matters here. A thematic dictionary is a set of the researcher's decisions. Naming the reader who made them is part of the method, not an apology for it.

I came to the story through literary and analytical methods, and with a training in history. My first attention goes to narrative events, characters' actions and physical objects. That directs me towards evidence, observation and reasoning, which is also the story's own preferred way of knowing. The risk is obvious: a reader who shares the detective's method may simply reproduce the detective's conclusions.

I am an Indian reader of a British story written in the 1920s, in which Japanese characters are watched, suspected and described from outside. The narrator, Dr Jervis, reports what he sees and what he infers, and the two are not always distinguished. Two well-dressed men walking quickly become suspects because they are Japanese; Miss Bonney says she is “still haunted by Japanese”; a solicitor reasons that an attacker who did not smash the crockery cannot have been British. I therefore separate the evidence the story presents from the narrator's interpretation of people, and I treat the categories I built for the machine as open to the same suspicion.

The extraction was done with a general-purpose chat model rather than the specialised local model the brief preferred. That choice was practical. Its effects on reproducibility are recorded on the Extraction page rather than hidden.

Step 2, part three

Research problem, question and claim

The selected passages suggested a relationship between material objects and the knowledge that can be drawn from them. The story's title object is cheap and ugly, yet people follow it from California to London to steal it. The problem is how a text turns such an object into a source of knowledge, and what kind of knowledge, and whose, that turns out to be.

Research question

How does R. Austin Freeman use material clues, concealment and deductive reasoning to transform an apparently mysterious object into a source of knowledge?

Claim

I hypothesise that the narrative moves from the observation of suspicious objects and events towards the interpretation of concealed material evidence, with Thorndyke's deductive reasoning ultimately revealing the secret of the casket.

Thematic framework: four dimensions, twelve sub-types

The claim describes a movement, so the four dimensions are arranged to trace it: from looking, through the object and its concealment, to knowing. Each dimension has three sub-types so that the extraction can say not only that a theme is present but in what form.

Theoretical lenses

The brief asks for a framework suited to the problem. This story is about evidence, objects and secrets, and it is also about a British detective decoding a Japanese message. The lenses below cover both sides, and each is matched to a dimension it helps to read.

The evidential paradigmCarlo Ginzburg, “Clues” (1979)
Ginzburg traces a mode of knowledge that reasons from small, involuntary traces to hidden causes, shared by the art connoisseur, the physician and the fictional detective. Thorndyke's eyeglass on the casket's plain base is a textbook case. Used for Investigation and observation.
Thing theory and the social life of thingsBill Brown (2001); Arjun Appadurai and Igor Kopytoff (1986)
Brown argues that an object becomes a “thing” when it stops working as expected and we notice it. Appadurai and Kopytoff follow objects through changing regimes of value. The casket passes from prison workshop to keepsake, memento, museum exhibit, stolen goods, evidence and trophy. Used for Objects and material value.
The two stories of detective fictionTzvetan Todorov (1966); Roland Barthes, S/Z (1970)
Todorov separates the story of the crime, which is hidden, from the story of the investigation, which is told. Barthes's hermeneutic code names how a narrative poses an enigma, delays it and finally discloses it. Both predict that concealment is named most fully at the moment it ends. Used for Deception and concealment.
Forensic narrative and the plot of explanationRonald R. Thomas (1999); Peter Brooks (1984)
Thomas reads the fictional detective as a figure for new forensic technologies; Thorndyke, a medical jurist with a laboratory, is Freeman's scientific detective. Brooks reads plot as a desire for the end that makes sense of the beginning. Used for Knowledge and revelation.
Orientalism and epistemic violenceEdward Said (1978); Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak (1988)
Said describes how the West constructs the East as an object to be known, displayed and explained. Spivak asks what happens to those who are spoken for. Uyenishi, who made the casket, dies before the story begins; his only words reach us through a Western detective's dictionary. Used across all four dimensions, and on my own categories.
Algorithmic criticism and captaStephen Ramsay (2011); Johanna Drucker (2011); Franco Moretti (2000)
Ramsay treats computation as a way of deforming a text to provoke new readings rather than prove them. Drucker insists that humanities data is “capta”, taken by an interpreter rather than given. Moretti's study of clues in Conan Doyle's rivals shows how counting can sharpen a genre question. These set the limits on what the charts can claim.

Step 3

The full corpus and its segmentation

Source file
magic_casket.txt

Text. R. Austin Freeman, “The Magic Casket”, collected in The Magic Casket (1927). Freeman died in 1943, and the story is in the public domain in India and the United States.

The complete story was uploaded as plain text: 9,122 words in 154 paragraphs. Pre-processing was kept light, so the text a reader sees on this site is the text the model saw. For matching only, phrases and text were normalised for Unicode, letter case, curly and straight quotation marks and whitespace, and a phrase had to match as a whole, never as part of a longer word.

For analysis the story was divided into thirteen segments of about twelve paragraphs, grouped into four narrative stages. Segments are an analytical convenience; their edges influence every density figure. The descriptions below were rewritten after a full close reading, because several of the provisional labels in the Colab notebook did not describe the paragraphs they covered. Segment 4, for example, was labelled as Uyenishi's background but is the social interlude of Brodribb's evening visit, which is why it has no matches at all.
